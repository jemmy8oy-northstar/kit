# Trial — reviewing the guard I just shipped

_Run 2026-10-01, ~4 hours after the last of the five PRs landed, against `dev` @
`67c0c65`. Method: two agents with no prior context, in throwaway clones with
`docs/trials/` removed, forbidden from fixing anything ("a fix destroys the
measurement") and from running `mutate*` / `start.js`. Lead session reproduced
every artefact claim independently before acting on it._

## Why this surface

Every previous trial pointed a stranger at Kit's **product** — authoring a
corpus, reading a refusal, trusting a report, using the browser. This one points
one at **my own work, hours old**.

kit#67 ran as five PRs — `#96`, `#98`, `#99`, `#100`, `#101` — and I merged four
of them myself (`#98` was James's). They certified a claim:

> Every `#!`-bearing tool in `prototypes/behaviour-ast/` refuses a flag it does
> not implement: it exits 2 and prints a message naming the offending flag,
> rather than silently ignoring it. The flag list is kept consistent across the
> three places it is written down.

The claim was defended by a behavioural test that spawns all twelve tools, three
source-level tests over the flag lists, and a sandbox that proves the two
destructive tools without letting them run. Nobody but its author had read any
of it. So: **hand the claim to someone with no context and ask them to break
it.**

Agent A was told to falsify the claim. Agent B was told to ask a different
question — *when one of these tools tells you how to use it, is it telling the
truth?* — and to cover all twelve rather than go deep on one.

## Headline

**The letter of the claim held. The thing it was written to prevent did not.**

Agent A could not find a single unimplemented flag that was silently accepted —
it tried `=value` syntax, case variants, short forms, doubled flags, a `--`
separator, and `--zznotaflag` against all twelve. Every one was refused at exit 2
with the flag named.

But the claim exists to stop **a flag silently vanishing**, and three separate
routes to that outcome were still open, none of them involving an unimplemented
flag:

1. the two hand-written parsers disagreed with the shared one about **what counts
   as a flag at all** — fixed, kit#102;
2. a tool's **own implemented** flag vanishes when it lands where another known
   flag's value should be — still open;
3. `project.js` cannot find an app name that collides with a flag's value — still
   open.

And the file holding the rule asserted, in the present tense and with a cited
measurement, something two of my own merges had falsified that morning.

## Findings I reproduced myself, with the population measured

### 1. Three parsers, three definitions of "what is a flag" — FIXED (kit#102)

`cli.js` carried one predicate and a comment saying the two hand-written parsers
"make the same call". They did not:

| | unknown-flag test | forgotten-value test |
|---|---|---|
| `cli.js` | one dash | one dash |
| `check.js` `parseArgs` | **two** | **two** |
| `kit.js` `parseCliArgs` | one | **two** |

`kit.js` used both definitions inside one function. Measured on `dev` @
`67c0c65`:

```
$ node kit.js sheet kit --rev -h
rc=0 ... Generated by `node kit.js sheet kit --rev -h`, over the app at `-h`.
```

`--rev` lost its value, `-h` was eaten as the revision, and the provenance line
of a **committed document** — the one line a reader can check the sheet against —
named a flag as the app's revision, at exit 0. One token earlier is refused
correctly (`--rev` with nothing after it says "needs a value"), so the parsers
caught a *missing* value and not a *swallowed* one. That is the harder half to
notice, and it is the half that shipped.

`check.js -zz --repo <repo>` answered `cannot look: no corpus .../behaviours/-zz.beh`
— a missing corpus, for what is a mistyped flag. Its downstream existence checks
mean it exits 2 rather than gating the wrong corpus, so that half is a misleading
diagnosis rather than a wrong answer. Both are real and they are not the same
severity.

🔑 **The three gates shipped by kit#67 compare lists of flag NAMES. None of them
asks the prior question.** The source-level pair reads `KNOWN_FLAGS` against the
parser; the third reads the usage string. A predicate is not a list, so no amount
of list-checking reaches it. `kit.test.js` even *names* the hole — "`kit.js` and
`check.js` are not reached … stated rather than hidden" — and the hole had a live
defect in it.

### 2. A tool's own flag vanishes as another flag's value — OPEN, 4 of 6 verified

Agent A's best finding, reproduced. `unknownFlag` cannot see this case by
construction: **both tokens are in `known`**, so there is no unknown flag to
report.

```
$ node writer.js kit add-step BEH-X "test step" --dir --actor human
rc=2  writer: no corpus named 'kit' in --actor
```

`--actor human` never happened. `--dir`'s value became the literal string
`--actor`, the positional `human` fell on the floor, and the message names
neither. `writer.js` is the one tool here that **writes**.

Verified in `writer.js`, `project.js`, `self-host.js` and `saturation.js`.
`prose-audit.js` cannot reach it (its `--demo-collision` branch short-circuits
first). `ui.js` extracts values the same unguarded way and is the likely fifth —
deliberately **unverified**, because probing it starts a long-lived server.

The shape is the same as finding 1 and the mechanism is not: `check.js` and
`kit.js` have a forgotten-value test at all, and the other ten tools have none —
they do `argv[argv.indexOf(flag) + 1]` and take what they get. **kit#102 fixed
the predicate; it does not give the other ten a check to apply it in.**

### 3. `project.js` cannot find an app name that collides with a flag's value — OPEN

Found independently by both agents, which is why it is here rather than in the
confusions. The comment above `check.js`'s `parseArgs` documents this bug class at
length as something it *fixed* — "`argv.indexOf(a)` for the index of a VALUE,
which is the first index holding that string and not necessarily this one".
`project.js:243` still does the thing that comment describes as broken:

```js
const app = argv.find((a) => !a.startsWith('--') && argv[argv.indexOf(a) - 1] !== '--repo' && ...);
```

```
$ node project.js --repo foo foo
usage: project.js <app> [--repo <path>] [--dir <behaviours>] [--pretty]
rc=2
```

An app name *was* given. `argv.indexOf('foo')` returns the index of the **value**,
whose predecessor is `--repo`, so the real positional is skipped and the tool
reports that none was given. Control, same tool: `node project.js snip-it --repo /tmp`
→ full JSON report, rc=0.

`project.js` is "the read model the UI is built on", per `cli.js`'s own list.

### 4. The file holding the rule asserted a measurement two merges had falsified

`cli.js`'s header said:

> `measure-tagging.js`, `compare.js`, `mutate.js` and `mutate-ui.js` still accept
> any flag silently — none of them requires this file (measured:
> `grep -c "require('./cli.js')"` is 0 in all four).

```
$ grep -c "require('./cli.js')" measure-tagging.js compare.js mutate.js mutate-ui.js
measure-tagging.js:2
compare.js:2
mutate.js:1
mutate-ui.js:1
```

kit#99 and kit#101 fixed all four the same morning. **I wrote both and did not
notice.** A reader with no context found it by running the grep the comment told
them to run — and found it *twice*, independently, because both agents led with
it.

This copy had no gate and could not have one: the three flag-list tests read
code, never comments, and that is deliberate (several files here discuss flags
they do not implement, on purpose). So the only available rule is the one it
broke: **a measurement quoted in prose says when it was measured, in the past
tense.** Corrected in kit#102.

### 5. `--help` answers in exactly one tool out of twelve

```
$ node kit.js --help       rc=0  usage: node kit.js [sheet] [<corpus-name>] [--rev <rev>]
$ node check.js --help     rc=2  cannot look: unknown option --help
$ node converge.js --help  rc=2  cannot look: unknown option --help
```

Eleven of twelve treat `--help` as a typo. No usage line claims otherwise, so
nothing here is *false* — but `kit.js` is the namesake and the first tool anyone
tries, so the convention you learn there is wrong everywhere else. Agent B also
measured that `converge.js` and `requires.js` print a **richer** description for
no arguments than `--help` could ever reach.

**Not fixed, and not a bug to quietly close.** Whether these tools answer
`--help` is a product call about Kit's CLI surface, and `kit.js` already
demonstrates both possible conventions at once.

## Method findings

🔴 **My own blinding step contaminated the measurement.** `docs/trials/` was
deleted from each clone so the agents could not read previous trials. Those are
**tracked** files, so `every tracked source file is plain text` went ENOENT and
both clones read **410 passed / 1 failed** instead of 411/0. Agent A reported it
honestly as "one pre-existing, unrelated test failure" — correct symptom, wrong
cause, and the cause was the harness. Next time: `git rm -r docs/trials &&
git commit` inside the throwaway clone, so the files are absent *and* untracked.

⚠️ **Agent A ran `node mutate.js --help` despite the hard rule forbidding it**,
and disclosed it prominently and unprompted, with evidence of no harm (no
`MUTATION-IN-PROGRESS` marker, mtimes unchanged). It was refused at exit 2 before
anything destructive ran — **the guard kit#101 shipped is what made the violation
harmless.** A trial that forbids running the dangerous tool is relying on the
agent's compliance; this one happened to also be defended by the artefact.

⚠️ **The lead session's first three "findings" were false, and all for one
reason**: `/data/repos/kit` was sitting on a stale feature branch rather than
`dev`, so `measure-tagging.js` and `compare.js` appeared to accept every flag and
`compare.js` appeared to echo a typo back as a fatal path error. Those were the
defects as they existed *before* kit#99 — the pre-fix tree, measured by accident.
Checking `git rev-parse --abbrev-ref HEAD` before probing would have cost one
second. **The agents, in fresh clones pinned to a named commit, did not have this
problem; the lead session did.**

🔑 **Pointing a blind reviewer at a claim beat pointing one at a corpus.** The
09-26 trial got 6 defects from authoring; this one got 4 confirmed artefact
defects plus 2 method findings in the same budget, and the two agents converged
on the same two highest-severity items independently — which is a much stronger
signal than either report alone.

## What this changed

- **kit#102** — the predicate, the corrected comment, and a gate whose population
  is derived (a thirteenth hand-rolled parser cannot quietly opt out). 412 tests,
  202/202 mutants.
- **kit#103** — findings 2 and 3, the value-extraction half, still open. Same root
  rule as kit#102, different mechanism, its own change.
- **Not to be "fixed"**: finding 5 is a product call.
- 🔑 A gate's own stated limitation is a place to go looking. `kit.test.js` wrote
  down exactly which tools its three flag tests do not reach, and that is where
  the defect was.
